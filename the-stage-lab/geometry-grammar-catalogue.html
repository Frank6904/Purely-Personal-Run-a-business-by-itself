The Geometry Grammar Catalogue

Twelve shapes, and everything each one silently argues. Every entry: what it is, why it works on the brain, how to build it, what it changes in the viewer, and the earning test that keeps you honest. Each geometry ships with a fillable worksheet and an editable PDF.

Why drawn shapes beat written words

Four laws sit under every entry in this catalogue. They are why a model taught as a drawing outlives the same idea taught as prose.

LAW 01
Twice encoded, twice recalled

A drawn shape with spoken narration is stored in two channels at once, image and language, and can be retrieved through either. Narrate while you draw, and put labels ON the drawing, never in a legend.

LAW 02
One shape, one chunk

Working memory holds about four things. A good geometry compresses many claims into one perceptual object: a loop turns six cause-and-effect sentences into a single spinning wheel. One idea per shape, six labeled elements maximum.

LAW 03
The reveal is load management

Viewers complete unfinished shapes in their heads, which is why drawing stroke by stroke, center last, layer by layer, holds attention. Progressive reveal is not theatre. It is how much the brain can take per second.

LAW 04
Every shape makes a silent claim

Up means better. Center means essence. Narrowing means loss. A loop means it compounds. The shape argues before you say a word, so the content must EARN the shape's claim. Every entry below has its earning test.

The Overlap

Interlocking circles whose intersection is the prize. The geometry of positioning and method.
01
Theory

Each circle alone is common; the overlap is rare, and the small area of the intersection encodes scarcity visually. It flatters ("your odd combination is the moat") and directs ("only compete where all circles hold"). Empty spaces between circles make the mind work, which makes the center reveal land harder.

How to build

Exclusion first: draw each circle and describe life with ONLY that circle (the near-miss diagnosis is the teaching gold). Center revealed and named last. In the full core-model version: the big promise anchors the center, three outcomes are the circles, nine signature moves ring the outside.

Outcome

Positioning clarity and permission to quit. Viewers drop activities that live in one circle only, and can finally say what makes their method theirs.

Earning test

The circles must be independent, and the center needs a qualification test, not a vague noun. If any two circles always come together, they are one circle.

The Pyramid

Stacked layers where each rests on the one below. The geometry of dependency, order and scarcity.
02
Theory

Three claims in one shape: hierarchy (top matters most), dependency (remove a base block and the top falls), scarcity (fewer fit higher up). It borrows architectural intuition: foundations first. The famous needs pyramid was never drawn by its author; the shape itself did the remembering.

How to build

Base first, always. Establish each layer as non-negotiable before drawing the next; the build order performs the dependency argument. The killer teaching move is removal: cross out a base layer and let the room watch the top collapse. The value version stacks Fix, Prevent, Improve, Transform with traffic-light scores.

Outcome

Sequencing discipline: "stop working on level three until level one is solid." And diagnosis: everyone can point at the broken layer.

Earning test

True dependency: would the top actually fall without the base? A list stacked for gravitas is the most common pyramid abuse. Five layers maximum.

The Mountain

A climb with a base, camps and a summit. The geometry of the guided journey.
03
Theory

A mountain pre-frames struggle: nobody expects a climb to be flat, so difficulty stops meaning failure. Camps make progress locatable ("you are here"), and the guide's authority is geometric: they have stood higher than the climber. The two-mountain variant (descend into a valley before the second, more meaningful climb) carries reinvention stories.

How to build

Base described in the climber's own words, no marketing language. Then the belief line: the exact belief that must break before the climb starts. Three camps, each a place to stand and look down at progress. The summit is a view, never a certificate: what becomes visible that could not be seen below.

Outcome

Expectation setting plus location. Redrawn every session with a moving dot, it becomes the program's progress bar and its retention device.

Earning test

The camps must be real standing points (a climber could stop there and still be better off), and the summit must show a view, not a credential.

The Three Roads

Diverging paths from one dot. The geometry of futures, urgency and honest closes.
04
Theory

Time compounds everything, good and bad, and diverging curves make that compounding visible. The grey middle road is held back and revealed only on resistance. The urgency belongs to the client, not the seller: the model does the confronting, the person stays kind.

How to build

Peg in the sand first (rule a line on the past, remove shame). Dot today, no judgement. Green road up with gates at 6, 12, 18 months; red road down, factual not frightening. Punchline: "right now you are as close to the green road as you will ever be." Then the guarantee rule: never the outcome, always the pathway.

Outcome

A decision that feels like the client's own. The strongest closing geometry there is, because it creates urgency without pressure.

Earning test

Every claim on the red road must be evidenced, and milestones on the green road must be numbers you can defend. If the client picks the grey road with open eyes, the model still did its job.

The Rings

Concentric circles around one center. The geometry of priority, intimacy and identity.
05
Theory

Inside means essence, outside means surface: the container schema does the arguing. Putting something at the center makes it feel causally prior, which is why the center must actually anchor the rings. The agency variant: your inner ring is what you control, and it grows when you work it, shrinks when you worry.

How to build

Fill from the center outward, but DRAW from the outside in when teaching: outer ring first (what everyone sees), center last with a pause, because the center is the reveal. Then the inversion move: "most people work from the outside in. We work from the center out." Always name what is outside the rings entirely.

Outcome

Identity-level reframe and prioritization by closeness. For offers: a belonging ladder where viewers locate themselves and see the ring inward.

Earning test

Does the center genuinely cause or anchor the outer rings? Four rings maximum, and a rings model with no named outside is a decoration.

The Four Rooms

Two crossed tensions making four territories. The geometry of sorting and deciding.
06
Theory

Two tensions at once force a dilemma instead of a ranking. Sorting things into space makes comparison perceptual rather than argumentative, and room nicknames convert positions into verdicts. An empty room reads as opportunity. The disarming entry is built in: "it's just a simple two by two."

How to build

Axes first, poles named so reasonable people could land on either end. Sort REAL items live before naming the rooms, then name them worst to best, target room last with an aspirational nickname. Reveal moves: subdivide the winning room, point at the center, or name the shameful hidden room everyone lives in.

Outcome

Decision. "It's complicated" becomes "there are four situations and each has one move." Also self-diagnosis: people instantly know which room they are standing in.

Earning test

The axes must be independent: if everything lands on the diagonal, one axis is fake. And you must be willing to discuss all four rooms, not just the good one.

The Funnel

A narrowing passage with numbered stages. The geometry of leaks and one-fix focus.
07
Theory

Narrowing communicates that loss is structural, not shameful, and the width difference between stages IS the conversion rate made visible. For the operator it converts anxiety into engineering. Know its limit: if customers feed the top again, the true shape is a loop, and the funnel becomes the diagnostic tool inside a flywheel story.

How to build

Wide mouth first with the biggest honest number. Stages named from the traveller's point of view ("they follow me", never "mid-funnel"). Real counts on every stage. Then circle the single biggest drop: the leak. The diagnostic power lives in the deltas, not the stages.

Outcome

One quarter, one leak, one fix. The viewer stops trying to fix everything and repairs the leakiest joint first.

Earning test

Stages must be states of the buyer's mind, widths must roughly match the numbers, and if the after-purchase energy feeds the top, draw the flywheel instead.

The Flywheel

A closed loop where every part drives the next. The geometry of momentum and compounding.
08
Theory

A loop has no terminal step, so the eye keeps traveling, and compounding is felt before it is explained. Circular causality also dissolves blame: no element is first, so the conversation shifts from whose fault to where to push. Spun backwards, the same shape shows the doom loop.

How to build

Four to six parts, clockwise, starting with the one you control most. The test between EVERY adjacent pair: "and then you almost can't help but..." Arrows carry the argument. Narrate two full laps to demonstrate compounding, then reverse it once to show decline. Name the fuel that accumulates in the center, the first push, and the biggest drag.

Outcome

Patience and consistency replace silver-bullet hunting. Effort gets reframed as feeding a wheel that gets easier every turn.

Earning test

The can't-help-but test between every pair, plus a real accumulator in the middle. A to-do list bent into a circle is the most common fake in the whole grammar.

The Iceberg

A visible tip, a waterline, and mass below. The geometry of hidden depth and meaning.
09
Theory

The waterline separates what gets talked about from what actually moves things, and the shape flatters while it implicates: "what you see is real, and it is the smallest part." Each level down offers more leverage and less visibility. The iceberg always moves in the direction of the base.

How to build

A striptease; order is everything. Waterline first. The tip carries their stated ask in their words. Then one level per reveal, with a pause: the want they would admit to, then the meaning only you can see. Finish with the fork ("shallow or deep?") and the base question ("do you want X, or do you want what X means?").

Outcome

The conversation leaves price and features and lands on meaning, where comparison shopping cannot follow. The signature belief-shift geometry.

Earning test

Each layer must be a deeper CAUSE of the one above, not a synonym, and the base must name something the client has never said out loud.

The Bridge

Two cliffs, a drop, and planks across. The geometry of safe passage through change.
10
Theory

The viewer feels the danger and the rescue in the same glance, which is why a drawn chasm paradoxically creates safety. The size of the gap IS the value of the change. Fear-only versions (set fire to the near cliff) decay fast; pair the drop with a destination worth standing on.

How to build

Near cliff in their verbatim words: diagnosis before prescription. Far cliff slightly higher and concrete. Name the drop once, kindly: what happens to people who jump without a bridge. Then the planks, three stages of your method, each one proven to have held someone before. Add the handrail: the support that never leaves.

Outcome

Change stops feeling like a leap and starts feeling like a walk. Your method's step order gets a visible reason to exist.

Earning test

The far cliff must be measurable, and every plank must answer a real obstacle, not restate your table of contents. A drop nobody believes kills the whole bridge.

The Ladder

Rungs of ascent, each earned by the last. The geometry of countable progress.
11
Theory

Up means more, and rungs make progress countable while keeping the next step small: one rung requires little courage. Each completed rung makes the next feel like continuation instead of a new decision. The same shape also audits thinking: walk a conclusion back down, rung by rung, to the facts it stood on.

How to build

Bottom up. Name each rung and its cost (money, time, or trust), then run the chasm check between every pair: if the step up is too big, a rung is missing. Mark the real entry rung and the stall rung, because the stall is your next offer or your next belief-shift to build.

Outcome

Ascension by design: the climber always sees exactly one small next step, and the builder sees where the ladder loses people.

Earning test

Evenly sized steps and no missing middle rung. A "small next step" that is secretly a chasm breaks trust permanently.

The Curve

Flat, dip, knee, climb. The geometry of time, expectation and not quitting too soon.
12
Theory

A curve converts present pain into a location on a shape that ends well. The vertical axis choice is the argument, so name it honestly. The dip filters out most people, which is exactly why the ones who know the dip is on the map keep walking. Story version: draw the arc WHILE telling a story, then place the listener on it.

How to build

Axes named out loud, then one continuous stroke, narrating the emotion of each segment. Mark "you are here": the moment the shape exists for. Then the two rules: the observable signal of the knee (what to watch for), and the difference between a dip worth crossing and a dead end worth quitting.

Outcome

The strongest persistence device in the grammar. Delays wrong quitting, speeds up right quitting, and forgives the flat start.

Earning test

An honest "you are here", a named y-axis, and a real answer to "how would we know this is a dead end, not a dip?" Without that answer the curve becomes a trap.

Running any worksheet

The same method fits all twelve. This is how the tools get finished instead of admired.

STEP 1
Solo, silent, twenty minutes

First pass alone, timer on, any order. Blank fields are findings, not failures: they show where the thinking is thin. The worked example button exists for the first pass; hide it on the second.

STEP 2
Compress last

Fill the long answers before the short labels. The five-word fields are the prioritization mechanism: if a label will not compress, the idea under it is not clear yet.

STEP 3
Share filled sheets, never fill together

In a group: everyone fills alone, then pairs compare drawings and steal the best labels. A model filled by committee belongs to nobody.

STEP 4
Close with a commitment

End every session on the debrief ladder: what do you notice, what surprised you, what does it mean, what will you do by Friday. The last thing written is a commitment, not a reflection.
